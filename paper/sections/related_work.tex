\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
For Bayesian state estimation, the relevant question is how a learned model combines temporal dynamics and measurements to represent uncertainty over the states. Learned estimators take one of two routes. A \emph{direct estimator} learns the conditional distribution of the state trajectory given the measurements, which enter the estimator through an encoder. Direct estimators include NormFlowSDE (Section~\ref{sec:method}), latent NSF (Section~\ref{sec:related-latent-nsf}), latent SDE (Section~\ref{sec:related-latent-sde}), and SigKer SDE (Section~\ref{sec:related-sigker}). A \emph{particle-filter estimator} learns a transition model and a measurement model separately and combines them by Bayesian weighting, so that the measurements enter only through the likelihood of the measurement model. Particle-filter estimators include NF-DPF (Section~\ref{sec:related-nfdpf}), NSF-PF (Section~\ref{sec:related-latent-nsf}), FlowSDE-PF (Section~\ref{sec:related-flowsde}), and NormFlowSDE-PF, which is included for completeness. Throughout, $x_k$ denotes the state, $y_k$ the measurement, and $u_k$ the control at step $k$.

\subsection{Direct Estimators}\label{sec:related-direct}
Conditional normalizing flows provide a route to Bayesian inference by learning a distribution over unknown quantities conditioned on observed data. For an unknown parameter vector $\vartheta$ and observations $y$, an invertible map $\vartheta=g_\phi(\epsilon;y)$ transforms a simple base variable $\epsilon\sim\N(0,I)$ into samples from a normalized conditional density $q_\phi(\vartheta\mid y)$, evaluated through change of variables \citep{dinh2014nice,rezende2015flows}. The invertible maps are compositions $g_\phi=f_{\phi,L_{\mathrm f}}\circ\cdots\circ f_{\phi,1}$ of $L_{\mathrm f}$ affine coupling layers \citep{dinh2017realnvp}. Each layer splits its input $\zeta$ into coordinate blocks $(\zeta_A,\zeta_B)$ and, given a conditioning input $c$, produces
\begin{equation}\label{eq:affine-coupling}
 \zeta'_A=\zeta_A,\qquad
 \zeta'_B=\zeta_B\odot\exp\bigl(\kappa_\ell(\zeta_A,c)\bigr)+\beta_\ell(\zeta_A,c),
\end{equation}
where $\odot$ denotes componentwise multiplication. The networks $\kappa_\ell$ and $\beta_\ell$, whose parameters are included in $\phi$, output the log scales and shifts.

Given training pairs from $p(\vartheta,y)=p(\vartheta)p(y\mid\vartheta)$, conditional maximum likelihood minimizes the expected forward KL divergence from the target posterior to its learned approximation \citep{radev2020bayesflow}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:amortized-forward-kl}
 \E_{p(\vartheta,y)}[-\log q_\phi(\vartheta\mid y)]
 =\E_{p(y)}\,\left[\KL\,\left(p(\vartheta\mid y)\,\Vert\,q_\phi(\vartheta\mid y)\right)\right]+C_0,
\end{equation}
where $C_0$ is the conditional entropy of $\vartheta$ given the observations, independent of $\phi$. Thus, paired simulations supply a posterior-learning objective without requiring pointwise evaluation of the target posterior. 

BayesFlow applies this principle to simulation-based parameter inference, combining a learned summary of the observations with a conditional invertible network \citep{radev2020bayesflow}. Once trained, it produces posterior samples for new datasets generated by the same simulator without per-dataset optimization. Its target is a fixed parameter vector, so the construction must be modified before it can infer an evolving state. \citet{rasul2021conditionedflows} condition normalizing flows on recurrent or attention-based representations of past observations and covariates and generate multivariate forecast trajectories autoregressively. In this formulation the flow learns a joint distribution of future states, current states, and past observations, which makes learning difficult and limits the forecast horizon.

The direct estimators in our benchmark share one interface. A causal encoder summarizes the measurements up to step $k$ in a context $c_k$, and a stochastic update produces $x_{k+1}$ from $x_k$ and $c_k$, starting from the known initial state. Repeated updates give $M$ sampled trajectories. The four estimators differ in the distribution of this update and in how it is trained. NormFlowSDE (Section~\ref{sec:method}) uses an observation-conditioned drift and a conditional normalizing flow on the noise of each update, trained on full rollouts. Latent NSF keeps the normalizing flow in the prior and estimates with a Gaussian posterior (Section~\ref{sec:related-latent-nsf}). The latent SDE (Section~\ref{sec:related-latent-sde}) and the signature-kernel SDE (Section~\ref{sec:related-sigker}) use Gaussian increments with a learned diffusion and are trained by a variational bound and by a proper scoring rule on paths, respectively.

\subsubsection{Neural Stochastic Flows and Latent NSF}\label{sec:related-latent-nsf}
Neural Stochastic Flows (NSFs) use a conditional normalizing flow to model the distribution of a future state $x_t$ given a starting state $x_s$ and a time interval $h=t-s>0$ \citep{kiyohara2025nsf}. The sampling procedure has two stages: a neural network constructs a Gaussian candidate $\widetilde x$, and an invertible network $g_\phi$ transforms that candidate into $x_t$. The context is $(x_s,h,s)$, with absolute time $s$ omitted for autonomous dynamics. The two sampling stages are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nsf-transition-sampling}
\begin{aligned}
 \widetilde x&=x_s+h\mu_\theta(x_s,h,s)+\sqrt h\,\sigma_\theta(x_s,h,s)\odot\epsilon,
 \qquad \epsilon\sim\N(0,I),\\
 x_t&=g_\phi(\widetilde x;x_s,h,s).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Here $\mu_\theta$ and $\sigma_\theta>0$ are outputs of ordinary multilayer perceptrons that set the Gaussian displacement and noise scale. The factors $h$ and $\sqrt h$ are written out although $h$ is also an input, so that $\widetilde x\to x_s$ as $h\to0$ whatever the networks output. The flow $g_\phi$ is a composition of affine coupling layers (Equation~\eqref{eq:affine-coupling}) conditioned on $(x_s,h,s)$, whose log scales and shifts are additionally multiplied by $h$, in contrast to Equation~\eqref{eq:affine-coupling}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nsf-affine-coupling}
 \zeta'_B=\zeta_B\odot\exp\bigl(h\,\kappa_\ell(\zeta_A,x_s,h,s)\bigr)+h\,\beta_\ell(\zeta_A,x_s,h,s).
\end{equation}
For $h>0$ the inverse transformation and its Jacobian give an evaluable transition density. Sampling evaluates Equation~\eqref{eq:nsf-transition-sampling} once for the whole gap $h$, and the Euler--Maruyama-type candidate is formed in a single step without SDE integration.

Transition densities of a Markov process satisfy the Chapman--Kolmogorov relation: a direct transition must agree with two successive transitions after averaging over the intermediate state \citep[Chapter~4, Section~1]{ethier1986markov}. NSF encourages its learned densities to satisfy this relation,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nsf-chapman-kolmogorov}
 p_\omega^{s,t}(x_t\mid x_s)
 \approx\int p_\omega^{r,t}(x_t\mid x_r)\,
               p_\omega^{s,r}(x_r\mid x_s)\,dx_r,
 \qquad s<r<t,
\end{equation}
where the approximation sign indicates that consistency is encouraged rather than guaranteed. The integral is generally intractable, so NSF introduces an auxiliary bridge distribution $q_\xi^{\mathrm{br}}(x_r\mid x_s,x_t;s,r,t)$ with parameters $\xi$, which yields variational upper bounds on the KL divergences between the direct and composed transitions in both directions. Averaging these bounds over sampled time triplets defines the consistency penalty $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{flow}}(\omega,\xi)$, and training on observed state pairs $\mathcal D$ minimizes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nsf-training-objective}
 \mathcal L(\omega,\xi)
 =-\E_{(x_s,x_t,s,t)\sim\mathcal D}
       \log p_\omega^{s,t}(x_t\mid x_s)
   +\lambda\mathcal L_{\mathrm{flow}}(\omega,\xi),
 \qquad \lambda\geq0.
\end{equation}

The NSF transition is a model of the dynamics. The published route from NSF to state inference is latent NSF \citep[Section~4]{kiyohara2025nsf}, a variational state-space model whose prior transition is an NSF and whose observations follow an emission model $p_\psi(y_k\mid x_k)$. Its inference network is a recurrent encoder of the past observations that outputs a Gaussian with diagonal covariance for each time,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:latent-nsf-posterior}
 q_\varphi(x_{t_i}\mid y_{\le t_i})=\N\,\left(x_{t_i};\,m_{t_i},\,\mathrm{diag}\,s_{t_i}^2\right),
 \qquad (m_{t_i},s_{t_i})=\mathrm{GRU}_\varphi(y_{t_i},\Delta t_i,h_{t_{i-1}}).
\end{equation}
Training minimizes a $\beta$-weighted negative evidence lower bound, which compares each posterior marginal with the NSF prediction from the previous posterior, together with the flow consistency penalty. In our adaptation the posterior is additionally conditioned on the previous sampled state and rolled out from the known initial state (Appendix~\ref{app:direct-baselines}).

\subsubsection{Latent SDEs}\label{sec:related-latent-sde}
Latent SDEs \citep{li2020scalable} model a time series through a prior SDE and an approximate posterior SDE that share their diffusion,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:latent-sde}
 \text{prior:}\quad dx=f_\theta(x,t)\,dt+g_\theta(x,t)\,dW_t,
 \qquad
 \text{posterior:}\quad dx=h_\varphi(x,c_t,t)\,dt+g_\theta(x,t)\,dW_t,
\end{equation}
where the posterior drift $h_\varphi$ also receives an encoding $c_t$ of the observations. Because the diffusions agree, Girsanov's theorem \citep[Sec.~8.6]{oksendal2003sde} makes the KL divergence between the two path measures finite \citep{li2020scalable},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:latent-sde-kl}
 \KL=\E\left[\int_0^T\tfrac12\|u_t\|^2\,dt\right],
 \qquad
 u_t=\frac{h_\varphi(x_t,c_t,t)-f_\theta(x_t,t)}{g_\theta(x_t,t)},
\end{equation}
where the expectation is over posterior paths. Training maximizes the evidence lower bound, the expected log likelihood of the observations under a decoder $p(y_{t_i}\mid x_{t_i})$ minus this path KL, with gradients through the SDE solver obtained by a stochastic adjoint. With a Gaussian decoder of learned variance, this objective can drive the variance towards zero. Since the posterior drift is conditioned on the encoded observations, posterior paths sampled from the initial state are observation-conditioned state trajectories. A latent SDE with a causal encoder is therefore a direct estimator. Its increments are Gaussian given the current state, so non-Gaussian marginals arise only from the nonlinearity of drift and diffusion.

\subsubsection{Neural SDEs Trained with Signature-Kernel Scores}\label{sec:related-sigker}
SDE-GANs \citep{kidger2021gan} train a neural SDE generator $dx=\mu_\theta(x,t)\,dt+\sigma_\theta(x,t)\,dW_t$ adversarially against a path discriminator. \citet{issa2023sigkernel} replace the discriminator by a scoring rule on path space. For a model path law $P$ and a realized path $x$, the signature-kernel score is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sigker-score}
 \mathcal S_k(P,x)=\E_{X,X'\sim P}\bigl[k(X,X')\bigr]-2\,\E_{X\sim P}\bigl[k(X,x)\bigr],
\end{equation}
where $X$ and $X'$ are independent generator paths and $k$ is the signature kernel. This kernel is the inner product of the path signatures after the paths are lifted with a static kernel, such as an RBF kernel. It is computed for a pair of discretized paths by solving a Goursat partial differential equation \citep{salvi2021goursat}. With a characteristic kernel the score is strictly proper, so its expectation over data is minimized only by the data-generating path law. Up to a term independent of $P$, it equals the squared maximum mean discrepancy. Given $m$ generator paths, both expectations have unbiased Monte Carlo estimates, and training needs neither a discriminator nor a min--max optimization. When the drift and diffusion also receive an encoding of the measurements and the score compares the generated paths with the paired reference states, the generator becomes a direct estimator of the state trajectory. As for the latent SDE, its increments are Gaussian given the state. However, it has no tractable transition density, since the score is evaluated only from samples.

\subsection{Particle-Filter Estimators}\label{sec:related-pf}
Bayesian filtering updates the distribution of a hidden state as new measurements arrive. A particle filter approximates this distribution, $p(x_k\mid y_{0:k})$, using $M$ candidate states, called particles, and weights describing their relative contributions. The bootstrap particle filter first propagates each previous particle through the transition model by drawing $x_k^{(i)}\sim p(\cdot\mid x_{k-1}^{(i)})$ \citep{gordon1993bootstrap}. The observation likelihood $p(y_k\mid x_k^{(i)})$ then measures how well each predicted state explains the measurement $y_k$, and the normalized weights are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bootstrap-weights}
 w_k^{(i)}=
 \frac{w_{k-1}^{(i)}p(y_k\mid x_k^{(i)})}
 {\sum_{j=1}^{M}w_{k-1}^{(j)}p(y_k\mid x_k^{(j)})}.
\end{equation}
The numerator combines information from earlier measurements, carried by $w_{k-1}^{(i)}$, with the new evidence. A resampling step then draws $M$ particles with replacement according to these weights and resets them to $1/M$. Some particle filters instead sample from a proposal $q$ that incorporates the new measurement, such as Gaussian proposals from local linearization \citep{doucet2000sequential} or the unscented particle filter \citep{vandermerwe2000unscented}. Each weight is then also multiplied by the ratio of transition and proposal densities, which equals one for the bootstrap filter. Exact filtering without particles is possible when the latent dynamics are restricted, as in Normalizing Kalman Filters, which combine an invertible observation transform with a linear Gaussian latent model \citep{debezenac2020nkf}. However, observation-likelihood training alone does not tie their latent coordinates to the physical states, so the filtered latent state is not an estimate of the physical state.

A learned particle filter needs a transition model that can be sampled and a measurement model whose density can be evaluated. NF-DPF learns its transition, proposal, and measurement distributions with normalizing flows by design, and its proposal is conditioned on the current measurement. For comparability in our benchmark, NormFlowSDE-PF, NSF-PF, and FlowSDE-PF are bootstrap filters. Their proposal is the learned transition itself, which is conditioned on the control and the time but not on the measurement, so the measurement $y_k$ enters only through the weights of Equation~\eqref{eq:bootstrap-weights}. Their measurement density $q_\psi(y_k\mid x_k)$ is a conditional normalizing flow with the same architecture and the same maximum-likelihood training in all three filters. Each filter fits its own instance jointly with its transition, so no trained network is shared between filters. Thus, they differ only in the transition model.

\subsubsection{Normalizing Flow-Based Differentiable Particle Filters}\label{sec:related-nfdpf}
\citet{chen2025flowparticle} introduce NF-DPF, which learns the transition, proposal, and measurement distributions using three invertible maps. Subscripts $\mathrm{dyn}$, $\mathrm{prop}$, and $\mathrm{obs}$ distinguish their separate parameter sets. With tractable base densities $g_{\theta_{\mathrm{dyn}}}$ and $g_{\theta_{\mathrm{prop}}}$, these models take the form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nfdpf-components}
\begin{alignedat}{3}
 \mathrm{Transition}&:\quad& x_k^{\mathrm{dyn}}&=f_{\phi_{\mathrm{dyn}}}(\zeta_k^{\mathrm{dyn}}),
 &\qquad\zeta_k^{\mathrm{dyn}}&\sim g_{\theta_{\mathrm{dyn}}}(\cdot\mid x_{k-1}),\\
 \mathrm{Proposal}&:\quad& x_k^{\mathrm{prop}}&=f_{\phi_{\mathrm{prop}}}(\zeta_k^{\mathrm{prop}};y_k),
 &\qquad\zeta_k^{\mathrm{prop}}&\sim g_{\theta_{\mathrm{prop}}}(\cdot\mid x_{k-1},y_k),\\
 \mathrm{Measurement}&:\quad& y_k&=f_{\phi_{\mathrm{obs}}}(\epsilon_k;x_k),
 &\qquad\epsilon_k&\sim\N(0,I).
\end{alignedat}
\end{equation}
The parameter vectors $\omega_{\mathrm{dyn}}=(\theta_{\mathrm{dyn}},\phi_{\mathrm{dyn}})$ and $\omega_{\mathrm{prop}}=(\theta_{\mathrm{prop}},\phi_{\mathrm{prop}})$ collect the base-density and flow parameters. The first map defines the transition density $p_{\omega_{\mathrm{dyn}}}(x_k\mid x_{k-1})$, the second the proposal $q_{\omega_{\mathrm{prop}}}(x_k\mid x_{k-1},y_k)$, from which the particles are sampled, and the third describes possible observations given a state. Invertibility makes all three densities evaluable by change of variables. For example, setting $\epsilon_k=f_{\phi_{\mathrm{obs}}}^{-1}(y_k;x_k)$ gives the measurement likelihood
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nfdpf-observation-likelihood}
 p_{\phi_{\mathrm{obs}}}(y_k\mid x_k)
 =p_\epsilon(\epsilon_k)\left|\det\frac{\partial f_{\phi_{\mathrm{obs}}}^{-1}(y_k;x_k)}{\partial y_k}\right|,
\end{equation}
where $p_\epsilon$ is the standard Gaussian density and $x_k$ is held fixed in the Jacobian. For each proposed particle $x_k^{(i)}\sim q_{\omega_{\mathrm{prop}}}(\cdot\mid x_{k-1}^{(i)},y_k)$, these densities enter the update
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nfdpf-weights}
 w_k^{(i)}\propto w_{k-1}^{(i)}
 \frac{\underbrace{p_{\phi_{\mathrm{obs}}}(y_k\mid x_k^{(i)})}_{\mathrm{measurement\ likelihood}}\,
 \underbrace{p_{\omega_{\mathrm{dyn}}}(x_k^{(i)}\mid x_{k-1}^{(i)})}_{\mathrm{transition\ density}}}
 {\underbrace{q_{\omega_{\mathrm{prop}}}(x_k^{(i)}\mid x_{k-1}^{(i)},y_k)}_{\mathrm{proposal\ density}}},
\end{equation}
followed by normalization as in Equation~\eqref{eq:bootstrap-weights}. NF-DPF uses entropy-regularized optimal-transport resampling to support gradient-based learning, and training maximizes a particle estimate of the observation log likelihood or minimizes a supervised state-estimation error. Although the proposal is conditioned on the current measurement, it is trained only as one factor of the importance weight in Equation~\eqref{eq:nfdpf-weights}, so its samples approximate the filtering distribution only after weighting by the transition and measurement densities and resampling. 

\subsubsection{FlowSDE-PF}\label{sec:related-flowsde}
FlowSDE uses conditional flow matching to train a neural vector field from state-transition data, together with a potential landscape for interpreting critical transitions \citep{lou2026flowsde}. The learned field is the drift of the SDE
\begin{equation}\label{eq:flowsde-related-dynamics}
 dX_t=f_\varphi(X_t,t)\,dt+\sqrt{2D}\,dW_t,
\end{equation}
where $W_t$ is standard Brownian motion and $D>0$ is a prescribed scalar diffusion coefficient \citep[Section~1.1]{lou2026flowsde_supp}. The flow-matching step constructs supervision for $f_\varphi$ between observed states. For a pair $(x_s,x_t)$ with gap $h=t-s$, it chooses an intermediate fraction $\tau\in(0,1)$ and draws a state at physical time $r=s+\tau h$ from the Gaussian bridge between the two observed states:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:flowsde-bridge}
 \widetilde X_r=(1-\tau)\,x_s+\tau\,x_t+\sigma_{\mathrm{br}}\sqrt{\tau(1-\tau)}\,\eta,
 \qquad \eta\sim\N(0,I).
\end{equation}
It is the marginal at time $r$ of a Brownian motion with scale $\sigma_{\mathrm{br}}$ pinned to $x_s$ at time $s$ and to $x_t$ at time $t$. Differentiating this bridge in time supplies an analytic vector-field target $b_r^{\mathrm{br}}$, expressed per unit physical time, and the data-fitting loss is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:flowsde-cfm-regression}
 \mathcal L_{\mathrm{CFM}}(\varphi)
 =\E\,\left[
 \left\|f_\varphi(\widetilde X_r,r)
       -b_r^{\mathrm{br}}(\widetilde X_r\mid x_s,x_t)\right\|^2\right],
\end{equation}
which is averaged over training pairs, intermediate times, and bridge samples. Flow matching thus turns dynamics learning into vector-field regression, without simulating trajectories or differentiating through a solver during training. A separate network represents a scalar potential $V_\eta$. It has no target of its own and is trained jointly with the drift through the regularizers of Equation~\eqref{eq:flowsde-training-objective} below, which make $-\nabla V_\eta$ the gradient part of the drift in the decomposition
\begin{equation}\label{eq:flowsde-potential-decomposition}
 f_\varphi(x,t)=-\nabla V_\eta(x)+R_{\varphi,\eta}(x,t),
 \qquad R_{\varphi,\eta}=f_\varphi+\nabla V_\eta,
\end{equation}
with a gradient component towards lower potential and a non-conservative residual force. The full objective,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:flowsde-training-objective}
 \mathcal L(\varphi,\eta)
 =\mathcal L_{\mathrm{CFM}}(\varphi)
 +\lambda_{\mathrm E}\mathcal L_{\mathrm{EPR}}(\varphi,\eta)
 +\lambda_{\mathrm H}\mathcal L_{\mathrm{HJB}}(\varphi,\eta)
 +\lambda_{\mathrm O}\mathcal L_{\mathrm{orth}}(\varphi,\eta),
\end{equation}
adds soft physical-consistency penalties with nonnegative weights: an entropy-production penalty on the residual force under weights derived from $\exp(-V_\eta/D)$, a Hamilton--Jacobi--Bellman penalty that encourages compatibility between the drift and the stationary density $p_{\mathrm{ss}}\propto\exp(-V_\eta/D)$, and a penalty on the alignment of $R_{\varphi,\eta}$ with $\nabla V_\eta$ \citep[Sections~1.2--1.4]{lou2026flowsde_supp}.

FlowSDE has no inference component. Its flow-matching targets are bridges between consecutive states, its uncertainty is fixed by the prescribed $D$, and its potential regularizers assume a stationary density of the autonomous dynamics. Conditioning the drift on measurements would leave the uncertainty fixed by $D$ instead of learning it from the observations, and would remove the interpretation of the potential. In FlowSDE-PF the drift is therefore conditioned on the state, the time, and the control, and particles are propagated by Euler--Maruyama integration of Equation~\eqref{eq:flowsde-related-dynamics}.
