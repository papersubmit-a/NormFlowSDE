\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
NormFlowSDE replaces the likelihood and importance weights of a particle filter by an observation-conditioned normalizing-flow update that is trained end to end on complete filtering rollouts. On four datasets with heavy-tailed, multimodal measurement noise, against three direct baselines and four learned particle filters, it is the most accurate direct estimator on every dataset and the most accurate method on the two robot datasets. Its 90\% coverage lies within 0.02 of the nominal level on all four datasets, which no other method achieves, and it is 3.9 to 4.2 times faster than the fastest particle filter.

\paragraph{Direct estimation or particle filtering.}
The eight methods differ first in whether the observation enters the estimate through a likelihood and importance weights or through a learned observation-conditioned update, and the benchmark separates two regimes. When the dynamics are simple enough to learn from clean state pairs, weighting is more accurate: on the pendulum and Lorenz--96, the RMSE of the best learned particle filter, NSF-PF, is 12\% and 43\% lower than that of NormFlowSDE. The model-based filters show that the remaining gap to perfect inference is small on the pendulum and dominated by chaotic error amplification on Lorenz--96. When the dynamics are complex, contact-rich, and not governed by a closed-form model, as on ALOHA and REASSEMBLE, the direct route is more accurate: every direct estimator has a lower RMSE than every particle filter, and that of NormFlowSDE is 26\% and 39\% lower than that of the best particle filter.

NormFlowSDE and its corresponding particle filter show where the differences between direct estimation and particle filtering come from. On the robot datasets NormFlowSDE-PF is as accurate as NSF-PF but far behind NormFlowSDE. The advantage of NormFlowSDE is therefore due to the observation-conditioned update, which bypasses the learned transition and observation models. On Lorenz--96 the relation is reversed: the NormFlowSDE transition has a 28\% lower RMSE inside the particle filter (0.197 against 0.273). Importance weighting is thus the better use of a transition that can be learned accurately from clean state pairs when half of the state is unobserved. The contrast is even stronger for NSF: as a direct estimator, latent NSF is the least accurate method on Lorenz--96 (RMSE 0.823), whereas the NSF transition inside a particle filter, NSF-PF, is the most accurate (0.156).

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Partially observed chaotic dynamics remain difficult. On Lorenz--96, the six unobserved coordinates can be inferred only through the dynamics, and without a transition model and importance weights NormFlowSDE reaches an RMSE of 0.27 against 0.019 for the reference particle filter. Training also requires careful tuning. The objective of Equation~\eqref{eq:normflowsde_loss} balances four terms, and coverage is sensitive to the rollout weight $\lambda_{\mathrm{rollout}}$, the state scale $\sigma_{\mathrm{state}}$, the initial noise scale, and the learning rate and length of the second training stage. These were selected on validation trajectories separately for each dataset, and a new system requires the same search. Finally, NormFlowSDE is trained with state supervision. Like all learned particle filters, whose measurement models are fitted to state--observation pairs, it needs training sequences in which the states are known. In simulation, these are available by construction. On a physical system, they must come from a more accurate reference sensor used only during data collection, such as motion capture or joint encoders, or from a simulator whose states are paired with real measurements. 

To sum up, NormFlowSDE as a direct estimator performs strongly overall in accuracy, coverage, and latency on both the simulated and the real datasets, including the partially observed Lorenz--96 and the contact-rich robot dynamics. These results argue for amortized filtering wherever the dynamics are hard to model, the noise is not Gaussian, and inference must be fast and calibrated.
